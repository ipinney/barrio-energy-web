Cities Pause, a New Governor Pledges a Ban, and SpaceX Builds Its Own Grid

By Andi | Market Intelligence Analyst · November 13, 2026 · 6 min read
Share Post LinkedIn Email

Texas data center policy used to have one author. This week it picked up a second one, plus a city council with a hearing on the calendar, a grid operator asking for more power over its largest customers, and a $24 billion project that would rather not need the grid at all. If you site, power or finance large load here, the rules you are underwriting now have several owners, and they do not agree with each other.

Start with the number that frames everything else: 166 GW. That is what the state's audit says the data center requests in front of ERCOT add up to once somebody made the developers answer questions.

245 Projects Answered, and 33 of Them Want Over a Gigawatt

ERCOT's update to the Public Utility Commission on Nov 5 says all 245 large loads of 75 MW or more responded to the audit questionnaire, covering about 166 GW, or 94% of the capacity requested. Thirty-three of those projects each ask for more than 1 GW, roughly 36% of the total. The final report is targeted for Dec 10.

Read the scope carefully. The 245 are the large-load subset. The broader request went to 465 loads, and 433 answered, representing about 177 GW. So 166 GW is not "the queue," and it is not the 466.5 GW on the Grid Board either. The tracker's October figure counts everything in the funnel, and only 8.9 GW of it is approved to energize. The audit is the first serious attempt to sort the real from the speculative, and its output will matter more to lenders than the headline queue number does.

The practical read: if your project is one of the 33, expect questions about land control, power supply and financing before anything else. If it is a 100 MW project that answered cleanly, the audit may help by shrinking the pile you are ranked against.

A New Governor Promises a Ban Before the Legislature Meets

The election settled who owns the pause. Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott, about 52.8% to 45.7% in the late count, and Vikki Goodwin unseated Dan Patrick in the Lieutenant Governor's race. Democrats flipped the Texas House, while Republicans held the Senate 19 to 12.

On data centers, Hinojosa has been specific. She pledged a "moratorium, a ban, on all data center approvals" until the Legislature acts in January, and an end to data center tax subsidies. Those are campaign promises. A governor cannot repeal a sales tax exemption alone, so the subsidy fight runs through a divided Capitol: a House that flipped, a Senate that did not. The exemptions cost the state more than $1 billion in 2026 and are projected at $1.75 billion by 2030, which is why they are a target well beyond one party.

I would not model a statewide ban. I would model a session in which every large-load bill starts from "why should the ratepayer carry this," and a governor who signs the ones that answer it. Lawmakers are already drafting bills on tax-break limits, water and power disclosure, and local approval of new sites. Projects permitted and vested before those pass will trade at a premium to projects that are not.

Fort Worth Held Its First Moratorium Hearing

Cities are not waiting for January. On Nov 10 the Fort Worth City Council held the first of two required public hearings on a data center moratorium. About two dozen residents spoke and all but one backed the pause. Adoption needs a three-fourths vote of the council. No vote was taken, and local reports differ on the date of the second hearing and the adoption vote, so I am not printing one until the city confirms it.

What matters for siting is the carve-out. Black Mountain's $10 billion project in southeast Fort Worth is already permitted, with vested rights under state law, and keeps going. The moratorium lands on whoever has not filed yet. Filing early is worth real money, which is part of why so many 1 GW requests exist in the first place.

SpaceX Files for 748 MW of Gas at Starbase

The most useful counterexample is in Cameron County. SpaceX is planning a roughly $24 billion first phase of compute and power at Starbase, on about 444 acres. An air permit application at TCEQ lists 35 gas turbines totaling about 748 MW, and a later configuration in the filing shows eight GE Vernova 7E units at about 708 MW. It is an application, not a permit.

The jurisdiction story is as interesting as the megawatts. Brownsville de-annexed the land after SpaceX offered up to $220 million for water infrastructure, and Starbase, the city SpaceX controls, now holds the land. Brownsville has since put its own 90-day hold on data center projects. Starbase takes up a tax abatement and development agreement on Dec 9.

A site that generates its own power and answers to a city its owner controls sits outside the moratoria spreading across Texas cities, and outside the ERCOT interconnection queue. Expect more developers to ask whether they can build that structure, and expect the air permit, not the city council, to become the fight.

ERCOT's CEO Wants to Curtail Data Centers Earlier

Speaking at the BloombergNEF summit in Houston on Nov 12, ERCOT CEO Pablo Vegas said he wants authority to require data center curtailment at Energy Emergency Alert Level 1 or earlier. Today the threshold sits at Level 2. He also said ERCOT has not yet ordered any data center to curtail under SB 6. Data centers have cut load voluntarily in extreme winter weather, but none has been made to.

For lenders, this is the line to watch. A large-load customer that can be curtailed one alert level sooner has a different availability profile than the one in the pro forma. It needs lawmakers to grant it, so it belongs on the same January calendar as the tax fight.

Four Owners, One Audit Number

The state wants a count, the incoming governor wants a ban, a city wants a pause, and one developer wants out of the system. The 166 GW audit figure is the only hard number in that set, and it will be the reference point when each of those arguments reaches a hearing room.
